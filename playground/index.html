An agent acted. Who said it could?

Every AI agent system on earth issues tokens. Almost none can answer, after the fact, which agent, under whose authority, and what exactly was authorized. agentid makes the answer structural — not forensic.

01

Three failures your tokens can't see

Agent identity today is a badge — a token that says "this is the marketing agent." Badges don't carry how far an agent may go, on whose behalf, or when the authority dies. So these happen, silently:

OVER-SPEND

The invoice nobody approved

Today: the marketing agent has meta-ads access. It makes a $4,500 purchase. The token was valid, so the ad ran. Finance finds it on the statement three weeks later.

With agentid: the grant capped the action at $2,000. The call is rejected before it reaches the ad platform — and the denial itself is a signed receipt.

OVER-READ

The PII it never needed

Today: the support agent can read the customer database — every record, every field — because that's what its role allows. It exports more than any single ticket required. Nobody notices.

With agentid: the delegation names one action, one system, nothing else. Reading outside it isn't denied-with-a-log — it's undeniably out of scope, rejected at the gate.

UNREVOCABLE

Revoked, but not really

Today: you offboard the contractor on Monday. Their agent's token lives until it expires on Friday. It keeps acting — for a user who no longer exists — until it dies of old age.

With agentid: revoke the leaf delegation and the very next tool call is denied. No waiting for expiry, no rotation ceremony — and a receipt names the denial.

02

Sign → gate → receipt

Three moving parts. No service to deploy, no sidecar to babysit, no database — one dependency, one CLI.

1

Sign a narrow grant

A human signs a delegation naming the one action, the one system, a hard cap, an approval threshold, and a 30-minute expiry. Signed once — not "clicked Allow" forever.

grant ad.credit.purchase · meta-ads
     cap $2,000 · cosign >$500 · 30m
→
2

Gate every call

Enforcement lives at the point of action, not login. Every single tool call re-checks the delegation against the profile — cap, budget, threshold, revocation. Miss a check, the action doesn't exist.

check(delegation, profile)
  → valid | invalid | indeterminate
→
3

Keep the receipt

Every outcome — executed or denied — is signed and hash-chained to the previous receipt. Later, anyone holding the inputs can re-derive the exact decision. Nothing to take on faith.

  chain: ✓  profile: sha256:9f2c…
Re-delegation only narrows — wider child grants are rejected at creation Budgets draw down across the chain Revocation bites on the next call, in-flight

The receipt answers the four questions

Whatever incident you get paged for at 2 a.m. boils down to four questions. agentid produces the answers as a matter of course — because the answers were the design constraint, not a forensics project.

Q1Which agent acted?spiffe://example.trust/agent/marketing-agent
Q2On whose behalf?oidc|acme-retail|usr_8842
Q3Under which credential?dlg-key-2026-11 · ES256
Q4Under whose approval?apr_01DEMO · cosign_above · $500

The outcome of any incident is a piece of paper. This implementation produces that paper as a matter of course — signed, chained, hash-verified, and re-derivable by anyone holding the inputs.

06

Don't re-trust — re-derive

Every decision this implementation makes is reproducible by any party holding the inputs. That property — not the interface — is the entire point of agent identity.

WHY NOW

NIST's NCCoE published a concept paper in February 2026 to close exactly this gap — agent identity, authorization, and audit for U.S. federal and critical-infrastructure use. The comment summary drew 614 responses; SP 800-63-4 explicitly excludes agents, and NISTIR 8587 names the risk and punts. agentid is one runnable, public answer — read the standards matrix and check our mapping.
